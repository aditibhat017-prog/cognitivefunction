# Literature Review

## Digital Biomarkers of Cognitive Function in Older Adults

### Background

Age-related cognitive impairment and dementia create a need for scalable approaches to monitoring cognitive health outside traditional clinical assessments. Digital biomarkers—objective, quantifiable behavioral or physiological measures collected using digital devices—may provide opportunities for continuous and ecologically relevant assessment of health and functional status.

Wearable sensors are particularly promising because they can continuously capture behaviors including physical activity, mobility, sleep, gait, and rest-activity patterns. These measurements may provide information related to cognitive health that is not captured through intermittent clinical testing.

### Physical Activity and Cognitive Function

Previous research generally supports an association between physical activity and cognitive function in older adults. Systematic reviews of objectively measured physical activity have found that greater physical activity and lower sedentary behavior tend to be associated with better cognitive performance.

Accelerometer-based studies are particularly relevant because they provide objective measurements of free-living physical activity rather than relying on participant self-report. Evidence suggests that total physical activity and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity are positively associated with cognitive outcomes, although reported effect sizes are generally modest.

Large meta-analyses examining longitudinal cognitive outcomes have similarly found that physical activity is associated with better late-life cognition and reduced cognitive decline, while emphasizing that the magnitude of these associations is relatively small.

### Wearable-Derived Digital Biomarkers

Wearable and portable technologies have increasingly been investigated as tools for identifying digital biomarkers associated with cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease. Candidate biomarkers include physical activity, gait characteristics, sleep, circadian rhythms, and other patterns of everyday behavior.

Reviews of digital biomarker research in mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease suggest that rest/activity measures are among the most frequently investigated wearable-derived biomarkers. However, substantial heterogeneity exists across devices, features, populations, and analytical approaches.

An important limitation of the existing literature is the relatively limited evidence demonstrating that wearable-derived biomarkers provide predictive information beyond readily available demographic and clinical characteristics. Independent validation and longitudinal studies also remain important areas for future research.

### Rationale for the Current Study

Based on the existing literature, objectively measured physical activity represents a plausible digital biomarker of cognitive function in older adults. However, the incremental information provided by wearable-derived activity measures beyond demographic characteristics remains an important question.

This project therefore evaluates whether wearable-derived physical activity features are associated with cognitive performance after accounting for age, sex, and education in adults aged 60 years and older.

NHANES 2013–2014 is used as the primary analysis cohort, with NHANES 2011–2012 serving as an independent replication cohort. This design allows the consistency of observed associations to be evaluated across two independent survey cycles.

### Connection to Preliminary Findings

In the NHANES 2013–2014 cohort, higher mean daily wearable-measured activity was associated with higher Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) performance after adjustment for age, sex, and education. The association was independently reproduced in the NHANES 2011–2012 cohort, although its magnitude and incremental explanatory value were smaller.

In contrast, relative day-to-day activity variability did not provide substantial additional explanatory value in either cohort.

These findings are consistent with previous literature suggesting a positive but modest relationship between objectively measured physical activity and cognitive function in older adults. They also motivate further investigation into whether wearable-derived features improve out-of-sample prediction of cognitive performance beyond demographic predictors alone.
